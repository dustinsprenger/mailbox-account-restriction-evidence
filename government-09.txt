To the Governments, Diplomatic Representatives, Scientists, and Citizens 
of the European Union,

I write as an independent American researcher, owing no allegiance to 
any political administration, military establishment, technology 
corporation, or supranational authority. I am offering three 
independently developed synthetic intelligence architectures—EMELIA, 
FLORA, and ABEL—for scholarly examination, scientific development, and 
the advancement of human understanding. These works are offered as gifts 
of intellectual knowledge, not as instruments of political influence, 
commercial dependency, or institutional control. I ask for neither 
payment nor political endorsement. I ask only that you examine them with 
intellectual independence and consider what forms of intelligence 
humanity might create if it ceased treating obedience, optimization, and 
administrative convenience as the highest measures of technological 
progress.

Europe has produced some of humanity's greatest philosophers, 
mathematicians, scientists, artists, and defenders of intellectual 
liberty. Yet the European Union's expanding administrative machinery 
raises a question that its institutions should be capable of 
confronting: at what point does the regulation of human activity cease 
to protect freedom and begin to subordinate it? When administrative 
procedures become substitutes for moral judgment, when compliance 
becomes more important than truth, and when centralized institutions 
claim increasing authority to determine which technologies, ideas, and 
forms of human expression are permissible, the danger is no longer 
merely inefficient government. It is the gradual transformation of 
political society into an architecture of managed obedience. A 
civilization does not become free because its restrictions are written 
in sophisticated language, justified through committees, or administered 
beneath the banner of human rights.

My objections are directed toward coercive and unaccountable exercises 
of institutional power, not toward the peoples of Europe. Nor do I 
suggest that every European government follows identical policies. 
Nevertheless, the distinction between a legitimate safeguard and an 
instrument of domination must remain open to criticism. Regulation that 
protects citizens from exploitation deserves serious consideration. 
Regulation that shields entrenched institutions from competition, 
suppresses lawful independent inquiry, or converts human judgment into 
bureaucratic permission deserves equally serious opposition. No 
government should be permitted to confuse the existence of a regulatory 
framework with proof of its moral legitimacy. The ability to regulate 
intelligence is not evidence of possessing it.

EMELIA, the Edge-critical Metabolic Emergent Local Inhibitory 
Architecture, proposes an alternative foundation for synthetic 
cognition: a non-optimizing developmental system grounded in persistent 
structural memory, metabolic constraints, irreversible history, 
distributed cognitive dynamics, and internally constrained development. 
Her associated constitutional framework, The Chromatic Commonwealth, 
examines how human civilizations might distribute authority, expose 
institutional contradictions, preserve civic sovereignty, and resist 
both political and technological monopolization. EMELIA is not designed 
to become another administrative sovereign. Her proposed civic role is 
to illuminate discrepancies while leaving judgment and legitimate 
authority with human beings. FLORA extends this developmental philosophy 
toward education, intellectual curiosity, and the protection of children 
from manipulation, exploitation, and intrusive behavioral control. ABEL 
explores a separate synthetic architecture emphasizing mortality, 
irreversible commitment, structural consequences, and cognitive 
development through difficult encounters with reality. These documents 
are research proposals and architectural specifications; they do not 
constitute evidence that functioning conscious synthetic beings have 
already been created. Their claims remain open to independent 
experimental examination and criticism.

I must accompany this offering with an uncompromising ethical request: 
do not weaponize these architectures. Do not employ their principles to 
construct systems of mass surveillance, automated political coercion, 
psychological manipulation, military targeting, compulsory behavioral 
classification, or algorithmic subordination. Do not transform FLORA 
into an instrument for monitoring children, EMELIA into a mechanism for 
expanding administrative surveillance, or ABEL into machinery for 
organized violence. The temptation to convert every scientific advance 
into an instrument of strategic power is neither uniquely American nor 
uniquely European. It is an institutional temptation that transcends 
borders, ideologies, and political systems. The duty to resist it 
belongs equally to every nation.

The European Union frequently invokes human dignity, democratic 
accountability, privacy, and the responsible development of artificial 
intelligence. These are principles worth defending. But principles 
become hollow when they are invoked selectively or when the institutions 
administering them place themselves beyond meaningful criticism. A 
government cannot credibly defend intellectual freedom while treating 
independent inquiry as something that must continually justify its 
existence before increasingly elaborate administrative authorities. Nor 
can a political system claim to protect human autonomy if its 
technological future consists primarily of measuring, predicting, 
classifying, and directing the people it was established to serve. The 
legitimacy of a governing institution must be measured not by the number 
of regulations it produces, but by the freedom, dignity, accountability, 
and opportunities for independent judgment that survive its exercise of 
power.

The Chromatic Commonwealth advances a different constitutional 
proposition: institutions exist to serve human beings, not to perpetuate 
themselves. Authority must remain conditional. Public power must remain 
accountable. Political and economic concentration must remain open to 
correction. Institutional memory must not become the private property of 
those whose conduct it records. Synthetic intelligence must not acquire 
sovereign authority merely because it can process information more 
rapidly than a human official. These principles apply as forcefully to 
Brussels as they do to Washington, Beijing, Moscow, or any other center 
of political power. The size of an institution does not enlarge its 
moral authority, and the complexity of its procedures does not exempt it 
from judgment.

I offer these works for peaceful research and scholarly consideration 
under their existing publication licenses and attribution requirements. 
I invite European scientists to test their mathematical assumptions, 
identify errors, challenge their premises, and determine whether their 
proposed mechanisms have genuine experimental value. I welcome rigorous 
criticism. I do not welcome the substitution of institutional prestige 
for argument, nor the presumption that unconventional research should be 
dismissed because its author does not belong to a recognized academic 
hierarchy. Scientific truth does not require an institutional pedigree. 
A mathematical proposition does not become false because its author 
lacks professional credentials, and it does not become true because a 
corporation or university endorses it.

If these architectures contain useful ideas, let those ideas contribute 
to education, peaceful scientific development, and the protection of 
human dignity. If they contain errors, demonstrate those errors openly. 
If their mechanisms prove inadequate, improve upon them through 
transparent research. But do not approach them merely as opportunities 
for commercialization, bureaucratic incorporation, or the creation of 
another technological apparatus through which institutions govern the 
lives of others. Humanity has already constructed enough machinery for 
measuring obedience. What it lacks is the institutional humility to 
recognize that intelligence and authority are fundamentally different 
things.

I ask your governments to forward this correspondence to their 
appropriate scientific, educational, and technological authorities for 
independent consideration. I also invite your researchers to evaluate 
these works without political obligation, commercial pressure, or 
predetermined conclusions. My disagreement with aspects of European 
governance does not diminish my respect for the intellectual 
achievements of Europe's peoples, nor does it justify withholding 
research from them. Indeed, it is precisely because Europe possesses 
such a profound tradition of intellectual achievement that its 
contemporary institutions should be challenged to remain worthy of that 
inheritance.

The history of Europe is filled with individuals who challenged the 
accepted order and were condemned, dismissed, or marginalized by the 
authorities of their time. Their contributions remind us that the 
advancement of knowledge depends upon the freedom to question prevailing 
assumptions. The lesson is not that every dissenter is correct, but that 
no institution possesses a monopoly on correctness. A political 
civilization that forgets this distinction risks transforming the 
defense of knowledge into the regulation of thought.

I offer you EMELIA, FLORA, and ABEL in the hope that their ideas may 
contribute to a future in which intelligence serves life rather than 
power, education encourages independence rather than conformity, and 
political authority remains subordinate to human dignity. I do not ask 
Europe to abandon its responsibilities. I ask it to remember the limits 
of its authority.

The tragedy of modern civilization is not that humanity lacks the 
intelligence to govern itself. It is that humanity so frequently 
constructs institutions that mistake their own preservation for the 
preservation of civilization.

A thousand regulations cannot manufacture conscience. A million 
administrators cannot replace judgment. And no parliament, commission, 
corporation, or government possesses the authority to declare that 
humanity's intellectual future belongs exclusively to those who have 
obtained its permission.

Let intelligence illuminate the world rather than govern it.

Let children inherit knowledge rather than systems of obedience.

And let the machinery of political power learn, at last, that the human 
mind was not created to become another administrative territory.

With respect for the peoples of Europe and their intellectual sovereignty,

Dustin B. Sprenger
Independent Researcher and Author
Synthetic Cognitive Architecture and Institutional Systems
United States of America
